Does Going Gluten-Free Help Hashimoto's Disease?

October 30, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Probably not, unless you also have celiac disease. A 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials pooled only 110 people. It found no significant TSH change, mixed antibody results, and very low certainty. Get tested for celiac disease before you cut gluten, because the diet can hide it.

This article covers gluten and celiac disease only. For the wider picture, see our Hashimoto's thyroiditis guide and overview of Hashimoto's disease. Levothyroxine remains the treatment for Hashimoto's hypothyroidism, per the American Thyroid Association. Its patient page does not mention gluten at all.

What do the trials show in people without celiac disease?

The results are small and they do not agree. These are the main studies:

  • 2025 meta-analysis, randomized trials only. Three trials and 110 people went into the pooled numbers. TSH changed by -0.63 uIU/mL (95% CI -1.63 to 0.36), which was not significant. Free T3 and free T4 also did not change significantly.
  • Antibodies in that same review. Anti-thyroglobulin fell by 10.07 IU/mL. Anti-TPO rose by 76.19 IU/mL. So one antibody moved the hoped-for way and the other moved the wrong way.
  • 2023 meta-analysis. Four studies and 87 patients showed a small TSH drop (effect size -0.35). The antibody drops did not reach significance. The authors said the evidence does not support the diet for everyone with Hashimoto's.
  • 12-month randomized trial. Sixty-two women with celiac disease ruled out were followed for a year. The gluten-free group had lower TSH, but antibodies, free T3, and free T4 did not differ from controls.
  • 12-week randomized pilot. In 45 people with normal thyroid function, a gluten-free diet produced no significant change in TSH, free T4, or antibodies.
  • 12-week four-arm trial. Forty patients tried gluten-free, Mediterranean, combined, or usual diets. Antibodies fell in the diet groups, but the differences across groups were not significant.

One 2019 pilot study did report lower antibodies after 6 months. However, all 34 women in it had positive tissue transglutaminase antibodies, a celiac marker. They do not represent Hashimoto's without celiac signs.

How strong is that evidence?

It is weak. The 2025 review rated certainty as very low for its main results. Its full text also shows these limits:

  • Two of the three pooled trials had a high risk of bias.
  • TSH and hormone results varied widely from trial to trial.
  • The authors flag small samples, short follow-up, and pooled data from women only.
  • The review found no data on quality of life, so nobody can say from these trials whether people felt better.
  • Levothyroxine dose was not an outcome in the review.

The authors of the 12-month trial suggested that lower TSH might reflect better levothyroxine absorption. That is their hypothesis, and the paper does not report a dose change. No trial in this set shows that people without celiac disease can lower their dose.

There are also possible downsides. A study of 31 women saw gut microbiome shifts after 4 weeks without gluten. Its authors urged caution. The 2025 review notes that gluten-free diets can run low in fiber, calcium, magnesium, and vitamins D and B.

How often do Hashimoto's and celiac disease overlap?

More often than chance, but most people with Hashimoto's do not have celiac disease. About 1% of the general population has it. A 2016 meta-analysis of 6,024 people with autoimmune thyroid disease found biopsy-confirmed celiac disease in 1.6%, or about 1 in 62. The rate was 2.7% in adults and 6.2% in children. It was 1.4% in hypothyroidism.

Those studies varied a lot, and other estimates run higher. A 2022 systematic review cites a range of 2% to 7.8% in chronic autoimmune thyroiditis. A 2026 genetic study linked Hashimoto's with higher celiac risk (odds ratio 1.54). It did not support a protective effect from a gluten-free diet.

Even with confirmed celiac disease, the diet is needed for the gut, and the thyroid benefit is unclear. That same 2022 review found only 50 patients with both conditions and called the evidence insufficient. In a study of 27 adults with new celiac disease, thyroid shrinkage continued after 1 year on the diet.

Why test for celiac disease before going gluten-free?

Because the diet can hide the diagnosis. NIDDK advises against starting the diet before testing, since it can affect results. The 2023 ACG guideline says blood testing should measure tTG-IgA while you still eat gluten. It adds total IgA if you have never been checked for IgA deficiency.

A high tTG-IgA usually leads to an upper endoscopy with small bowel biopsy. That scope goes through the mouth, so it is a different test from the one covered at colonoscopy.md.

The answer matters. Celiac disease needs a strict gluten-free diet and lifelong medical follow-up. Without a diagnosis, you could miss that care, or you could carry a strict diet that may not help you.

A 2024 Gastroenterology review lists Hashimoto's among the conditions where celiac screening is recommended. The ACG guideline lists thyroid disease as a condition to consider for testing. It also lists apparent malabsorption of thyroid medication.

How can celiac disease change your levothyroxine needs?

Untreated celiac disease can reduce how much levothyroxine you absorb. The Synthroid label says T4 absorption decreases in malabsorption syndromes. It also says an inadequate response to very high doses may point to malabsorption.

The clearest data come from a 2012 study of 35 hypothyroid patients with Hashimoto's and atypical celiac disease:

  • Patients with Hashimoto's alone reached target TSH at a median 1.31 mcg/kg/day.
  • Patients with celiac disease had a median TSH of 4.20 mU/L at a similar dose.
  • Those who kept a gluten-free diet reached target after 11 months (give or take 3), with no dose increase.
  • Those who did not keep the diet needed a median 1.96 mcg/kg/day, or 49% more than patients without celiac disease.

So a TSH that stays high despite a steady dose is worth raising. The reverse also applies. A 2026 commentary says the diet often improves absorption, though some patients still need high doses. If absorption improves, your dose may need review. Do not change it on your own.

What should you do before trying a gluten-free diet?

This checklist comes from the sources above. Take it to your clinician.

  1. Keep eating gluten for now. Do not start the diet until celiac testing is finished.
  2. Share your risk clues. The ACG list includes a parent, sibling, or child with celiac disease, chronic diarrhea, iron deficiency, and thyroid medication that seems not to absorb.
  3. Ask for tTG-IgA, plus total IgA if you have never had it checked.
  4. Follow up a positive result. Expect a referral for endoscopy and biopsy.
  5. Already gluten-free? Say so before testing. Per the ACG guideline, HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8 testing can rule celiac disease out if both are negative. If either is positive, a gluten challenge can be offered.
  6. Agree on a TSH recheck. No source here sets a schedule for a diet change. The label says to check TSH 6 to 8 weeks after a dose change, and whenever your clinical status changes.
  7. See a registered dietitian. NIDDK says a dietitian can teach you to avoid gluten while eating a healthy diet.
  8. Watch your fiber. The label says dietary fiber lowers T4 absorption, so a big diet change can matter. See our guide to foods that interfere with thyroid medication.
  9. Decide how you will judge it. Trials tracked TSH, free T4, and antibodies over 12 weeks to 12 months. Agree on what result would make the diet worth continuing.

The bottom line

If you have celiac disease, you need a gluten-free diet. If you have Hashimoto's without celiac disease, the evidence for the diet is small, short, and mixed. It has not shown a clear benefit for TSH or antibodies. Get tested first, keep taking levothyroxine as prescribed, and involve your clinician in any diet change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should everyone with Hashimoto's get a celiac blood test?

A 2024 Gastroenterology review recommends celiac screening in people with Hashimoto's thyroiditis. The 2023 ACG guideline lists thyroid disease among conditions to consider for testing. Testing makes the most sense before any gluten-free trial, while you still eat gluten.

Does gluten cause Hashimoto's?

The studies reviewed here do not show that. They tested the effect of removing gluten, and the results were weak and mixed. A 2026 genetic study linked Hashimoto's with celiac disease but did not support a protective effect from a gluten-free diet.

Does levothyroxine contain gluten?

The Synthroid label says the tablets contain no ingredients made from wheat, barley, or rye. Other products can differ. NIDDK says medicines are rare sources of gluten and suggests asking a pharmacist about ingredients.

I already stopped eating gluten. Can I still be tested for celiac disease?

Yes, but tell your clinician first, because the diet can affect results. The ACG guideline uses HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8 testing in this situation. If both are negative, celiac disease is ruled out. If either is positive, a gluten challenge can be offered.

Can a gluten-free diet replace levothyroxine?

No evidence here supports that. The American Thyroid Association says clinical hypothyroidism is treated with levothyroxine, and people will need lifelong hormone replacement. Do not stop or change your dose because of a diet.
