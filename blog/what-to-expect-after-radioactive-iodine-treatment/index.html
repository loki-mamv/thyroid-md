What Should You Expect After Radioactive Iodine Treatment for Hyperthyroidism?

October 18, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: After radioactive iodine (I-131) for an overactive thyroid, you follow home radiation precautions for a few days to about two weeks. Most people feel better within 4 to 8 weeks. Many, especially people with Graves' disease, then become hypothyroid and start daily thyroid hormone. Expect blood tests every 4 to 6 weeks for the first 6 months, and plan to wait at least 6 months before trying to get pregnant.

This article covers radioactive iodine for hyperthyroidism from Graves' disease or toxic nodules. It does not cover the larger doses used after thyroid cancer surgery. If you are still choosing a treatment, see our guide to hyperthyroidism symptoms and treatment.

What radiation precautions do you follow at home?

For a short time after treatment, your body gives off radiation, and traces of I-131 are in your urine and saliva. That is why the precautions focus on distance, bathrooms, and shared items. Federal rules let a clinic send you home only if the expected dose to anyone else is low. Under 10 CFR 35.75, the clinic must give you written instructions when another person could receive more than 1 mSv.

The American Thyroid Association patient page lists the usual precautions:

  • Sleep in a separate bed, about 6 feet from another adult, and away from a pregnant partner, child, or infant.
  • Keep about 6 feet from children and pregnant women, and limit time in public places.
  • Delay your return to work, and avoid planes, public transport, and long car trips with others.
  • Do not prepare food for others or share utensils.
  • Drink plenty of fluids, sit to urinate, and wipe the toilet seat after use.

The ATA says these precautions typically last 3 days to 2 weeks. The exact length depends on your dose and home situation. The 2016 ATA hyperthyroidism guideline says the treating doctor should give written advice on precautions. If you cannot follow them, the guideline says a different treatment should be chosen. The ATA's separate radiation safety recommendations give doctors tables to set the number of days for each patient. Ask for your own number of days before you go home.

If you travel in the days after treatment, carry a letter from your doctor. The ATA notes that airport and federal building detectors can pick up very small amounts of radiation.

What side effects can show up in the first few weeks?

Most people have few physical side effects. The ATA says I-131 can cause mild pain in the neck. Over-the-counter pain relievers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen usually help. The guideline describes radioactive iodine as well tolerated, with rare complications other than eye disease.

Hyperthyroid symptoms can briefly get worse. The guideline notes that RAI can cause a short-term rise in thyroid hormone levels. Thyroid storm, a dangerous surge, is rare after treatment. People at higher risk, such as older adults or people with heart disease, may restart antithyroid medicine a few days after RAI. Beta-blockers for a fast heart rate are often continued until hormone levels fall.

Dry mouth, taste changes, and sore throat do happen after radioactive iodine. The ATA lists them as effects of thyroid cancer treatment, which uses much larger doses. They are not typical after the lower doses used for hyperthyroidism. Report any new tightness in the neck, trouble breathing, or trouble swallowing right away. The guideline says neck swelling after RAI is very rare but needs prompt review.

How long until thyroid levels change?

Radioactive iodine works slowly. The ATA says it may take several months to reach its full effect. For Graves' disease, the guideline says most people have normal thyroid tests and fewer symptoms within 4 to 8 weeks.

The guideline recommends a check of free T4, total T3, and TSH within the first 1 to 2 months. Tests then repeat every 4 to 6 weeks for 6 months, or until you are stable on thyroid hormone. TSH can stay low for months after hormone levels improve, so doctors read it with free T4 and T3.

Toxic nodules respond on a similar timeline. For a single toxic nodule, the guideline reports a 75% response rate by 3 months and 89% by 1 year. For a toxic multinodular goiter, response is 50% to 60% by 3 months and 80% by 6 months.

Treatment does not always work the first time. For Graves' disease, the guideline suggests a second treatment if hyperthyroidism persists after 6 months. For multinodular goiter, about 20% of people need retreatment.

How likely are you to need levothyroxine afterward?

For Graves' disease, becoming hypothyroid is usually the goal. The guideline says the aim of RAI is to control hyperthyroidism by making the patient hypothyroid. NIDDK says almost everyone who gets radioiodine therapy later develops hypothyroidism. Daily thyroid hormone treats it, and NIDDK notes it causes fewer long-term health problems than hyperthyroidism.

Timing varies. The guideline cites data showing 40% of Graves' patients were hypothyroid by 8 weeks and more than 80% by 16 weeks. The change most often comes between 2 and 6 months. Your doctor starts thyroid hormone based on your blood tests and symptoms. Our levothyroxine guide covers how the medicine is taken and monitored.

Toxic nodules are different. RAI mostly targets the overactive nodule, and the rest of the thyroid often keeps working. In one study of 684 people with a single toxic nodule, hypothyroidism rose from 7.6% at 1 year to 28% at 5 years and 60% at 20 years. For multinodular goiter, one large study found 3% at 1 year and 64% at 24 years. So yearly thyroid tests matter, even if you feel fine.

Can radioactive iodine affect your eyes?

Yes, for people with Graves' disease. Radioactive iodine can bring on new thyroid eye disease or make existing eye disease worse. The guideline notes that complications of RAI are rare except for those tied to the eyes. Smoking raises the risk. Some people with mild active eye disease receive a short steroid course with RAI to protect the eyes.

We cover the trial data, risk factors, and steroid prevention in our article on thyroid eye disease after Graves' treatment. Report new eye pain, redness, puffiness, or double vision after treatment.

How long should you wait before pregnancy?

RAI is never given during pregnancy or breastfeeding. The guideline recommends a pregnancy test within 48 hours before treatment for anyone who could be pregnant. Breastfeeding should stop well before treatment and should not restart afterward. The ATA says breastfeeding is safe after future pregnancies.

Sources give slightly different waiting times:

  • The 2016 ATA guideline says women should delay conception until thyroid levels are stable on thyroid hormone. This typically takes 4 to 6 months or longer. It lists RAI as a good fit for women planning pregnancy more than 6 months after treatment.
  • The ATA patient page says to delay pregnancy until at least 6 to 12 months after I-131.
  • For men, the guideline says to delay conception for 3 to 4 months to allow for sperm turnover.

The guideline found no evidence of reduced fertility once thyroid levels are normal. Children of treated patients show no excess birth defects. If you plan a pregnancy, tell your doctor early, because thyroid targets change in pregnancy.

Recovery also has an emotional side. Shifts from high to low thyroid levels can affect sleep, mood, and energy. Tracking how you feel between blood tests on symptom.md can help you give your doctor a clear picture.

The bottom line

Radioactive iodine is a common and effective treatment for an overactive thyroid. Expect a short period of home radiation precautions, usually 3 days to 2 weeks. Then come slow improvement over 1 to 2 months and regular blood tests for about 6 months. If you have Graves' disease, plan on becoming hypothyroid and taking daily thyroid hormone. If you have a toxic nodule, thyroid function may stay normal for years, but keep up with yearly tests. Wait at least 6 months before pregnancy, and watch for any new eye symptoms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Am I radioactive after radioactive iodine treatment?

For a short time, yes. The amount drops quickly over the first days as the I-131 decays and leaves the body. That is why precautions such as distance from children and separate beds usually last only 3 days to 2 weeks.

Can I hug my children after radioactive iodine?

Brief contact is usually limited during the precaution period. The American Thyroid Association advises keeping about 6 feet from children and pregnant women during that time. Ask your treating doctor for the exact number of days that applies to your dose.

Why do I feel worse a few weeks after radioactive iodine?

Thyroid hormone can rise briefly after treatment, and symptoms improve slowly over 4 to 8 weeks. Later, tiredness, weight gain, or feeling cold can mean levels have dropped too low. A blood test tells the two apart.

Will I need a second dose of radioactive iodine?

Most people need only one treatment. For Graves' disease, the ATA guideline suggests a second treatment if hyperthyroidism lasts beyond 6 months. About 20% of people with toxic multinodular goiter need retreatment.

Can radioactive iodine for hyperthyroidism cause cancer?

The American Thyroid Association says some studies suggest a slightly higher risk of thyroid cancer after RAI for hyperthyroidism. The absolute risk is small. Discuss your own risk with your doctor if it concerns you.
