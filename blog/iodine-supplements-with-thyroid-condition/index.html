Should You Take Iodine Supplements If You Have a Thyroid Condition?

The short answer: Usually not. Most US adults already get the 150 mcg a day they need from food. The American Thyroid Association advises against iodine or kelp supplements above 500 mcg a day, and says people with thyroid disease are more prone to harm from excess iodine. Pregnancy is the main exception.

How much iodine do adults need each day?

Your thyroid uses iodine to make its hormones. The body cannot make iodine, so it has to come from food or supplements.

The MedlinePlus page on iodine in the diet lists the Recommended Dietary Allowance from the National Academies:

  • Adults and teens age 14 and older: 150 mcg a day
  • Pregnancy: 220 mcg a day
  • Breastfeeding: 290 mcg a day
  • Children 9 to 13: 120 mcg a day; children 1 to 8: 90 mcg a day

The ceiling matters as much as the target. The ATA statement on excess iodine sets the adult Tolerable Upper Limit at 1,100 mcg a day and says intake above it "is not recommended and may cause thyroid dysfunction." It also says there is "no known thyroid benefit of routine daily iodine doses in excess of the U.S. RDA."

Food covers the target easily. MedlinePlus puts 78 mcg in a quarter teaspoon of iodized salt and 146 mcg in a 3 ounce portion of cod. Dairy, bread, and seafood are other common sources.

Do most Americans already get enough iodine?

Yes, as a population. A CDC analysis of national survey data from 2005 to 2008, published in Thyroid in 2011, measured iodine in urine. The authors concluded that iodine nutrition in the general US population has been stable and adequate since 2000.

The same report flagged gaps. Pregnant women had lower levels, and 56.9% of those surveyed fell below the World Health Organization cutoff for pregnancy. Levels were also lower among non-Hispanic Black participants. So the national average is fine, but some groups fall short.

The ATA statement agrees that "the U.S. diet generally contains enough iodine" for non-pregnant adults. For most people with a thyroid condition, the question is not how to get more iodine. It is how to avoid getting far too much.

Why can extra iodine hurt a thyroid that is already sick?

A healthy thyroid adjusts to a wide range of iodine intake. A damaged or overactive gland may not. A 2014 review in Nature Reviews Endocrinology says iodine above the daily need is generally well tolerated. But people with pre-existing thyroid disease, older adults, and fetuses and newborns are at higher risk of iodine-induced thyroid problems.

The same review notes that excess iodine can cause either hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, mild or overt. The source of the extra iodine "might not be readily apparent," which is why supplements are easy to overlook.

For Hashimoto's, the NIDDK page on Hashimoto's disease is direct. People with autoimmune thyroid disease "may be sensitive to harmful side effects from iodine." Large amounts from kelp, dulse, seaweed, or iodine-rich medicines "may cause hypothyroidism or make it worse," and iodine supplements "can have the same effect." NIDDK also suggests asking your care team about cough syrups that may contain iodine. Our Hashimoto's thyroiditis guide covers the condition itself.

Iodine also interacts with antithyroid drugs. MedlinePlus warns that high doses of iodine taken with antithyroid medicines can have an additive effect and could cause hypothyroidism.

Are kelp and seaweed supplements a safe way to get iodine?

They are an unreliable one. The ATA warns that many iodine, potassium iodide, and kelp supplements contain iodine "up to several thousand times higher" than the daily upper limit.

A 2021 study in Food & Nutrition Research tested 96 seaweed products sold in Norway. Supplements ranged from 5 to 5,600 mcg of iodine per daily dose. One portion of whole seaweed ranged from 128 to 62,400 mcg. For 54 of the products, one portion or dose would exceed the upper limit. The authors also found that the iodine declared on several labels was inadequate or inaccurate.

That spread is the core problem. Two kelp products can differ by a factor of a thousand, and the label may not tell you which one you have.

What changes in pregnancy and breastfeeding?

Pregnancy reverses the usual advice. The baby gets iodine from your diet, so you need more. The ATA recommends that women take a multivitamin with 150 mcg of iodine a day, as potassium iodide, before conception, during pregnancy, and while breastfeeding.

The NIDDK page on pregnancy and thyroid disease says the same thing: experts recommend a prenatal vitamin with 150 mcg of iodine, "especially if you don't use iodized salt." It also warns that too much iodine from supplements such as seaweed can cause thyroid problems.

The ceiling still applies in pregnancy. The ATA advises against more than 500 mcg a day from supplements during pregnancy and breastfeeding. NIDDK notes that too much iodine can cause a goiter in the baby. Check the prenatal label for the iodine line and its amount. For hormone targets, see our post on how pregnancy changes thyroid targets.

Why do some thyroid patients need less iodine, not more?

Some patients are told to cut iodine on purpose. The ATA radioactive iodine page says your doctor may ask you to follow a low iodine diet before radioactive iodine treatment for thyroid cancer.

The ATA low iodine diet guide explains that the diet helps the treatment work better. It lists these to avoid:

  • Vitamins or supplements that contain iodine, "especially kelp and dulse"
  • Iodized salt, sea salt, and ingredients listed as iodates, iodides, or algin
  • FD&C Red Dye #3, which can appear in candies, cereals, and vitamins
  • All herbal supplements when you are not sure how much iodine they contain

The guide also says to ask your doctor before stopping any red-colored medicine or any iodine-containing medicine, such as amiodarone. Your care team sets how long the diet lasts. Our post on what to expect after radioactive iodine treatment covers the weeks that follow.

How do you read a supplement label for iodine?

Federal labeling rules for supplements (21 CFR 101.36) shape what you can and cannot learn from the box. Work through these steps:

  1. Find iodine in the Supplement Facts panel. It sits between phosphorus and magnesium in the required mineral order. Note the amount in mcg per serving.
  2. Check the serving size. If a serving is two or three capsules, the iodine you take per day may be higher than it first looks.
  3. Look at the source. The rule allows the source in parentheses after "as" or "from." "Potassium iodide" is a measured form. "From kelp" depends on a plant whose iodine content varies widely.
  4. Watch for a proprietary blend. A blend gives one total weight for its herbal and other ingredients, not the amount of each. Kelp, bladderwrack, or dulse inside a blend can hide the real dose.
  5. Add up every product. A multivitamin, a "thyroid support" formula, and a prenatal vitamin can each contain iodine.
  6. Compare with the limits. Over 500 mcg a day from supplements is above the ATA advice. Over 1,100 mcg a day total is above the upper limit.
  7. Bring the bottle to your visit. NIDDK suggests asking your care team whether you should take iodine at all.

Salt matters too. MedlinePlus calls iodized salt the main food source of iodine. If you cut salt to protect your blood pressure (see hypertension.md), ask whether your iodine intake still meets the daily need.

The bottom line

If you have a thyroid condition and are not pregnant, you likely do not need an iodine supplement. US diets generally supply the 150 mcg adults need. Extra iodine, especially from kelp, can push a diseased thyroid toward hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are the exception, where the ATA advises 150 mcg a day as potassium iodide. Before radioactive iodine for thyroid cancer, the goal is less iodine, not more.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does iodized salt count toward my daily iodine?

Yes. MedlinePlus calls iodized salt the main food source of iodine and puts about 78 mcg in a quarter teaspoon. MedlinePlus advises checking that the salt you buy is labeled iodized.

Can iodine supplements cure Hashimoto's?

No. NIDDK says large amounts of iodine from supplements or seaweed may cause hypothyroidism or make it worse in people with Hashimoto's. The ATA says there is no known thyroid benefit of routine iodine doses above the US recommended amount.

Is it safe to eat sushi or seaweed snacks with a thyroid condition?

Seaweed iodine content varies widely, and one 2021 study found single portions of whole seaweed ranging from 128 to 62,400 mcg. Occasional small amounts are different from daily kelp tablets. If you have Hashimoto's or are preparing for radioactive iodine, ask your care team what limit fits you.

Do I need iodine if I take levothyroxine?

Levothyroxine replaces the hormone your thyroid would make, and the ATA does not recommend routine iodine above the daily allowance for adults. Pregnancy is different: the ATA advises a multivitamin with 150 mcg of iodine a day before conception, during pregnancy, and while breastfeeding.

Why is iodine restricted before radioactive iodine treatment?

The ATA says a low iodine diet helps radioactive iodine treatment for thyroid cancer work better. The diet removes iodized salt, dairy, seafood, kelp, and iodine-containing supplements for a period your care team sets.
