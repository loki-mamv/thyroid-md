Why Does My TSH Keep Changing When I Take the Same Dose?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: TSH is a jumpy number. In a 2022 European study of healthy adults sampled weekly, TSH varied about 17.7% within the same person, versus under 5% for free T4. Time of day, absorption, biotin, new medicines, and life changes add more swing. One odd result is often noise; a trend is a signal.

Is some TSH change normal on a steady dose?

Yes. TSH comes from the pituitary gland. It rises when thyroid hormone is low and falls when it is high, as MedlinePlus explains. Because the pituitary reacts strongly to small shifts in T4, TSH moves much more than the hormone it tracks.

The European Biological Variation Study (EuBIVAS) measured this in 91 healthy adults, sampled weekly. The within-person variation was 17.7% for TSH, 4.8% for free T4, and 5.0% for free T3. Those people did not change anything. Their TSH still drifted week to week.

Here is what that looks like in practice. If your usual TSH is 2.0 mIU/L, 17.7% of that is about 0.35. So readings of roughly 1.7 one month and 2.3 the next can come from ordinary biology, with no change in your thyroid or your pills. On top of that, each lab test has its own small measurement error. For what each value on the report means, see thyroid blood tests explained.

Does the time of the blood draw matter?

More than most people expect. TSH follows a daily rhythm. A 1995 study in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism describes a pronounced nighttime rise in TSH. The same rhythm was present in people with hypothyroidism taking levothyroxine.

The rise carries into the morning and then falls. A 2019 analysis of hospital lab data found that TSH dropped from 7 a.m. to 1 p.m. Compared with 7 a.m. draws, later results ran 7% to 25% lower. Free T4 and free T3 barely moved with the clock.

A small 2013 study of 22 patients on levothyroxine shows why this matters. Their median TSH was 3.27 mU/L in the morning and 2.18 mU/L in the afternoon. By the morning samples, 45.5% looked undertreated. By the afternoon samples, only 9% did. Same people, same dose, same day.

What changes how much levothyroxine you absorb?

The Synthroid prescribing information says the gut absorbs 40% to 80% of an oral dose. That wide range is why habits matter. The label notes these points:

  • Fasting increases absorption. The label says to take the dose on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast.
  • Soybean flour, cottonseed meal, walnuts, and dietary fiber can bind the drug. Grapefruit juice can delay absorption.
  • Proton pump inhibitors, antacids, and sucralfate lower stomach acid and can reduce absorption.
  • Other drugs that block absorption should be taken at least 4 hours apart from levothyroxine.
  • Absorption may decrease with age.

Small drifts in routine add up. Coffee with the pill one week, a new fiber habit the next, or a few missed doses can each nudge TSH. Levothyroxine has a half-life of 6 to 7 days in people with normal thyroid levels, per the label, so missed or doubled doses blur together over a week or two. The full list of food and supplement conflicts is in supplements and foods that interfere with thyroid medication.

Biotin is a special case. It does not change your thyroid. It can distort the test itself. The label says biotin may interfere with immunoassays for TSH, T4, and T3, and advises stopping biotin and biotin-containing supplements for at least 2 days before the blood draw. Many hair, skin, and nail products contain it.

Can a new pharmacy refill change your TSH?

Possibly, but less often than people fear. Pharmacies can switch between generic manufacturers without telling you. A 2022 JAMA Internal Medicine study compared 2,780 matched pairs of adults on a stable generic dose. Normal TSH after the index date was 84.5% in people who switched generics and 82.7% in those who did not. The authors found no clinically significant TSH change.

The authors note that this conflicts with guideline advice to watch for TSH changes after a switch. A cautious middle path: check your pill color and manufacturer at each refill. If it changed and your next TSH looks off, tell your clinician. That detail helps them decide whether to repeat the test or adjust.

Which life changes shift your dose needs?

Sometimes TSH moves because your body changed, not the pill. The label lists factors that set the right dose:

  • Body weight. Full replacement doses are calculated per kilogram (about 1.6 mcg/kg/day for many adults). A large gain or loss can shift the need.
  • Pregnancy. The label advises a dose increase and a TSH check every 4 weeks until stable. It also says to return to the pre-pregnancy dose right after delivery.
  • Estrogen. Oral estrogens and estrogen-containing birth control raise thyroxine-binding globulin, the protein that carries T4 in the blood. The label tells clinicians to factor this in when reading results.
  • New medicines. The label lists many drugs that affect absorption, binding, or thyroid function.
  • Illness. MedlinePlus notes that serious illness unrelated to the thyroid can lower TSH for a short time.
  • Age. The label notes that absorption may decrease with age.

Heart health changes the pace, too. The label advises lower starting doses and slower steps for adults with heart disease or atrial fibrillation risk. If you manage blood pressure alongside your thyroid, hypertension.md covers that side.

When does a TSH change matter?

Timing is the first filter. The label says to recheck TSH 6 to 8 weeks after any dose change, and peak effect may take 4 to 6 weeks. A test drawn sooner may not show where you will settle. On a stable dose, the label suggests checking every 6 to 12 months, or whenever your health changes.

Use this checklist to make the next result easier to read:

  1. Book the draw at the same time of day as last time, ideally the same lab.
  2. Stop biotin at least 2 days before, per the label.
  3. Keep your dose routine steady for the 6 weeks before the test.
  4. Note any new medicines, supplements, or pill color change since the last test.
  5. Note big life changes: weight, pregnancy, estrogen, or a recent illness.
  6. Compare against your last two or three results, not just the reference range.

A change matters more when it is large, keeps going the same direction across tests, or comes with symptoms. Racing heart, tremor, and sleeplessness can point to too much hormone; see signs your levothyroxine dose is too high. Fatigue, cold intolerance, and weight gain point the other way. A single reading slightly outside your usual band, with no symptoms, is often worth repeating before anyone adjusts.

The bottom line

TSH swings more than the hormones it tracks, about 17.7% within the same healthy person. Draw time can move it by a quarter. Absorption habits, biotin, new medicines, and life changes add more. Standardize the test, track the trend, and give dose changes 6 to 8 weeks before judging them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I take levothyroxine before my blood test?

The levothyroxine label does not set a rule on this. Many clinicians ask patients to keep the same routine before each test so results compare fairly. Ask your prescriber which they prefer and stick with it.

How much can TSH change from day to day?

In the EuBIVAS study of healthy adults, TSH varied about 17.7% within each person across weekly samples. Time of day can add more. A study of hospital lab data found afternoon results up to about 25% lower than 7 a.m. results.

Does a generic levothyroxine switch always change TSH?

No. A 2022 JAMA Internal Medicine study of adults on a stable generic dose found no clinically significant TSH change after switching manufacturers. Still, tell your clinician if your pills changed before an unexpected result.

How long should I wait to recheck TSH after a dose change?

The levothyroxine label says to check TSH 6 to 8 weeks after any dose change in adults. Levothyroxine has a half-life of about a week, so earlier tests may not show your settled level.

Can stress or a cold change my TSH?

Serious illness unrelated to the thyroid can lower TSH for a short time, per MedlinePlus. A minor cold is less likely to matter much. If you were sick near your test date, mention it so your clinician can decide whether to repeat it.
