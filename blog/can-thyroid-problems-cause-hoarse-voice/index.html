Can a Thyroid Problem Cause a Hoarse Voice?

September 28, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, but it's not the most common cause. An underactive thyroid, a large goiter, thyroid cancer, and thyroid surgery can all change your voice. Most hoarseness comes from colds and throat infections and clears within about 2 weeks. Hoarseness that lasts more than 4 weeks, or that comes with a neck lump, trouble breathing, or trouble swallowing, needs a look at your vocal cords.

How is the thyroid connected to your voice?

Your thyroid sits in the lower front of your neck, just below the voice box (larynx). Two nerves on each side run close to it. The recurrent laryngeal nerve moves the vocal cord. The external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve controls a muscle that helps you project your voice and reach high notes, according to the American Thyroid Association (ATA).

That closeness is why thyroid problems can reach the voice in two ways:

  • Hormone effects. Low thyroid hormone can change the tissue of the vocal folds.
  • Physical effects. An enlarged gland, a nodule, a cancer, or surgery can press on or injure the nerves that move the vocal cords.

Hoarseness itself means your voice sounds weak, breathy, scratchy, or husky, or its pitch changes. MedlinePlus notes that most hoarseness comes from a problem with the vocal cords themselves. It lists both an underactive thyroid and thyroid cancer among the less common causes.

Does hypothyroidism make your voice hoarse?

It can, mostly when hypothyroidism is untreated for a long time. MedlinePlus lists hoarseness among the late symptoms of untreated hypothyroidism, along with slow speech and a puffy face, hands, and feet. Early symptoms are more often fatigue, feeling cold, constipation, and weight gain. You can read more in our guide to hypothyroidism symptoms and warning signs.

The voice changes seem to track how low thyroid hormone actually is. A 2022 study in Auris Nasus Larynx compared 26 people with subclinical hypothyroidism, 26 with overt hypothyroidism, and 30 people with normal thyroid function. The overt group had a lower voice pitch and rated their own voice problems higher. The subclinical group did not differ from the controls. The authors concluded that hypothyroidism does not seem to affect the voice much until thyroxine (T4) levels fall. If your TSH is only mildly high, see our page on subclinical hypothyroidism.

How it happens is less settled than many websites suggest. The common explanation is that the vocal folds thicken and swell, much like the puffiness seen elsewhere in the body. A 2025 rat study found more type I and type III collagen in the vocal folds of hypothyroid animals, but no change in hyaluronic acid. That supports a real tissue change, but animal data do not prove the same process in people.

The practical point: hoarseness from hypothyroidism usually shows up with other signs of low thyroid. A new hoarse voice by itself, in someone whose thyroid levels are well treated, has a different cause until proven otherwise.

Can a goiter or thyroid nodule press on your voice box?

Yes. A goiter is any enlargement of the thyroid gland, and it can occur whether the gland makes too much, too little, or the right amount of hormone, per the ATA. When a goiter grows large, MedlinePlus says it can press on nearby structures. That can cause:

  • Hoarseness
  • Cough
  • Trouble swallowing, especially solid food
  • Trouble breathing with very large goiters, especially when lying flat or raising your arms

Most thyroid nodules are small and cause no symptoms at all. The ATA's thyroid cancer page notes that a nodule large enough to compress the windpipe or esophagus may cause trouble breathing or swallowing, or a "tickle in the throat." Our thyroid nodules guide covers how nodules are checked.

Is hoarseness a sign of thyroid cancer?

Sometimes, but not often. Most thyroid cancers show up as a painless lump or are found on imaging done for another reason. The ATA says that, less commonly, hoarseness can develop if a thyroid cancer invades the nerve that controls the vocal cords.

When a nerve stops working, one vocal cord can become paralyzed. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) lists lung or thyroid cancer, tumors of the neck or chest, and injury to the neck among the causes of vocal fold paralysis. Symptoms include a hoarse or breathy voice and trouble breathing.

Hoarseness plus a firm neck lump is a combination to take seriously. That is exactly the kind of case where a voice box exam should not wait.

Why does your voice change after thyroid surgery?

Voice change is one of the main known risks of thyroidectomy, because the operation works right next to the voice nerves. The ATA gives these figures:

  • Temporary hoarseness: a 5% to 7% risk. It gets better on its own as the irritated recurrent laryngeal nerve recovers.
  • Permanent nerve injury: a 0.5% risk. Even then, the voice can often be brought back to normal with additional procedures.
  • Superior laryngeal nerve injury: low risk, but it can change your singing voice and your ability to yell or project.

The ATA says the most common voice change after surgery is a deeper voice, from scarring of the muscles and nerves outside the voice box. A weak vocal cord can also let thin liquids like water slip toward the airway, so coughing when you drink is worth reporting. Nerve monitoring during surgery can predict nerve function, but it does not prevent injury.

If you are having thyroid surgery, ask your surgeon how your voice will be checked before and after the operation.

When does hoarseness need a laryngeal exam?

The American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery sets the threshold in its 2018 hoarseness (dysphonia) guideline. Clinicians should perform laryngoscopy, or refer to someone who can, when hoarseness does not resolve or improve within 4 weeks. They should do it sooner, whatever the duration, if a serious underlying cause is suspected. Laryngoscopy uses a small scope to look directly at the vocal cords.

The same guideline says CT or MRI should not be ordered for a voice complaint before someone has looked at the larynx. In other words, the scope comes first. A thyroid ultrasound or scan may follow if the exam points toward the thyroid.

MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider sooner if:

  • You have trouble breathing or swallowing
  • Hoarseness has lasted more than 2 to 3 weeks in an adult, or more than 1 week in a child

If you are not sure whether your symptoms fit a thyroid problem at all, symptom.md can help you sort through possible causes before your visit.

The bottom line

A thyroid problem can cause a hoarse voice. Untreated hypothyroidism, a large goiter, an invasive thyroid cancer, and thyroid surgery are the main routes. Still, colds and throat infections are the most common cause of hoarseness. If hoarseness lasts past 4 weeks, or comes with a neck lump, breathing trouble, or swallowing trouble, ask for a laryngeal exam. A thyroid blood test can rule hypothyroidism in or out, but it cannot replace looking at the vocal cords.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my voice go back to normal once my thyroid levels are treated?

If low thyroid hormone is the cause, the voice may improve once hormone levels are back in range. If your voice does not improve after your levels are in range, another cause needs to be checked.

Can Hashimoto's disease cause a hoarse voice?

Hashimoto's can cause hoarseness in two ways. It can lead to hypothyroidism, and it can enlarge the thyroid into a goiter. Either one can affect the voice, but a new hoarse voice should still be checked for other causes.

Can an overactive thyroid change my voice?

A goiter can form with an overactive thyroid too, as with Graves' disease, and a large goiter can press on nearby structures. Voice changes from hyperthyroidism itself are much less clearly described than those from hypothyroidism.

How soon after thyroid surgery should my voice recover?

The American Thyroid Association says temporary hoarseness after surgery happens in about 5% to 7% of patients and improves as the nerve recovers. Permanent nerve injury is rare, at about 0.5%. Tell your surgeon if your voice is not improving or if you cough when drinking water.

Which doctor should I see for hoarseness?

Start with your primary care doctor, who can check your thyroid levels and examine your neck. For hoarseness lasting more than 4 weeks, or sooner if something serious is suspected, you need an exam of the vocal cords. That is usually done by an ear, nose, and throat specialist.
